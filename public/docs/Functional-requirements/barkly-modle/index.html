The Barkly Model

A Replicable Framework for Compassionate, Sustainable, Human-Centered Organizations

Version: 0.1 — Initial Concept Originating Organization: Barkly Labs Founder: Piper Core Principle: People are people, not numbers.

1. Definition

The Barkly Model is an organizational framework for designing, operating, and evaluating enterprises and institutions that seek to combine economic sustainability with meaningful human, animal, and community well-being.

It proposes that organizations can be designed to create economic value while simultaneously improving the conditions in which people live, work, participate, and thrive.

The model is intended to be:

Modular: Adaptable to different industries and organizational types.

Replicable: Implementable by independent organizations.

Measurable: Evaluated through economic, social, and operational outcomes.

Evolvable: Improved through evidence, experience, and community feedback.

Accessible: Designed with diverse human needs and abilities in mind.

Transparent: Open to scrutiny, evaluation, and responsible criticism.

Sustainable: Designed for long-term viability rather than short-term gains.

The Barkly Model is not a requirement to create a particular corporate structure. It is a framework for how organizations can be designed and operated.

2. The Seven Foundational Principles

Principle I — Human Dignity

Every person possesses inherent worth independent of economic productivity, employment, disability, income, or social status.

Organizations should treat people as individuals rather than merely as labor, customers, statistics, or financial resources.

Implementation:

Respect individual autonomy and dignity.

Avoid unnecessarily discriminatory or exclusionary practices.

Recognize diverse needs and circumstances.

Ensure people have meaningful opportunities to participate.

Treat employees, customers, and community members with respect.

Principle II — Adaptive Accessibility

Organizations should adapt their systems to accommodate the people interacting with them.

Accessibility is a fundamental design consideration, not an optional accommodation added after exclusion has occurred.

Implementation:

Integrate accessibility into products, services, and workplaces.

Accommodate diverse physical, cognitive, sensory, and psychological needs.

Identify and reduce unnecessary barriers.

Provide multiple ways to access services and participate.

Evaluate accessibility continuously.

Principle III — Compassionate Economics

Financial sustainability and human well-being should be treated as interconnected organizational objectives.

The model encourages organizations to investigate how ethical treatment, accessibility, trust, and long-term investment in people can contribute to economic performance.

Implementation:

Evaluate long-term consequences alongside immediate financial returns.

Invest in employee well-being and development.

Avoid unnecessary exploitation.

Pursue financially sustainable operations.

Measure economic performance alongside social outcomes.

Principle IV — Community Integration

Organizations should recognize that they exist within communities and have relationships with the people affected by their operations.

Implementation:

Engage with affected communities.

Encourage meaningful participation.

Build mutually beneficial partnerships.

Support community resilience where appropriate.

Consider community needs in organizational decision-making.

Principle V — Individual Autonomy

Support should enable people to make decisions about their own lives.

Organizations should avoid unnecessary paternalism, coercion, and restrictions on individual choice.

Implementation:

Respect informed decision-making.

Offer choices whenever reasonably possible.

Provide support without unnecessary control.

Protect privacy and personal information.

Recognize different definitions of independence and success.

Principle VI — Responsible Innovation

Innovation should be evaluated according to both its capabilities and its consequences.

Implementation:

Consider potential harms and unintended consequences.

Prioritize safety, accessibility, and privacy.

Use evidence to guide development.

Seek meaningful public benefit where appropriate.

Maintain accountability for organizational decisions.

Principle VII — Long-Term Sustainability

Organizations should be designed to endure and remain capable of fulfilling their purpose over time.

Implementation:

Develop sustainable revenue and funding.

Maintain appropriate financial reserves.

Invest in infrastructure and organizational capacity.

Reduce dependence on single points of failure.

Plan for leadership transitions and continuity.

Balance growth with organizational stability.

3. The Five-Layer Organizational Framework

The Barkly Model organizes organizational design into five interconnected layers.

Layer 1 — Purpose

Defines why the organization exists and whom it seeks to serve.

Questions:

What problem are we addressing?

Who experiences this problem?

Why does it matter?

What values guide our decisions?

What would meaningful success look like?

Deliverable: A documented mission, vision, and set of foundational principles.

Layer 2 — People

Defines how the organization interacts with employees, customers, participants, and communities.

Questions:

Who interacts with the organization?

What needs and barriers do they experience?

How can their dignity and autonomy be protected?

How can they meaningfully participate?

What support and accommodations are necessary?

Deliverable: A human-centered operational and accessibility framework.

Layer 3 — Operations

Defines how the organization actually functions.

Questions:

What products or services does it provide?

How are decisions made?

What processes are necessary?

How are resources allocated?

How are safety, quality, and accountability maintained?

Deliverable: A documented operational system.

Layer 4 — Economics

Defines how the organization sustains itself financially.

Questions:

How does it generate or obtain revenue?

What are its costs and financial risks?

How does it compensate people?

How does it reinvest?

How does it maintain financial stability?

What resources can support its mission?

Deliverable: A sustainable financial and resource-allocation model.

Layer 5 — Impact

Defines how the organization evaluates its effects on people, communities, and the environment.

Questions:

Are people's lives meaningfully improving?

Are the organization's economic objectives being achieved?

Are unintended harms occurring?

Are outcomes equitable and accessible?

What should change based on the evidence?

Deliverable: An impact measurement and continuous-improvement framework.

4. The Barkly Organizational Cycle

The Barkly Model uses a continuous improvement cycle.

Step 1 — Identify

Identify a meaningful problem experienced by people or communities.

Step 2 — Understand

Research the problem, affected individuals, existing solutions, and underlying causes.

Step 3 — Design

Develop an accessible, ethical, and practical organizational solution.

Step 4 — Implement

Introduce the solution through a pilot, service, enterprise, or program.

Step 5 — Measure

Evaluate financial performance, operational effectiveness, and human outcomes.

Step 6 — Learn

Identify successes, failures, limitations, and unexpected consequences.

Step 7 — Improve

Modify the organization based on evidence and feedback.

Step 8 — Share

Publish appropriate findings, documentation, and lessons so others can learn.

Then repeat the cycle.

5. The Barkly Dual-Outcome Standard

Organizations adopting the Barkly Model should evaluate two primary categories of outcomes.

A. Economic Performance

Depending on the organization:

Revenue and revenue growth.

Profitability and operating margins.

Operating costs and efficiency.

Financial reserves and stability.

Employee retention.

Customer satisfaction.

Innovation and competitiveness.

Long-term viability.

B. Human and Community Outcomes

Depending on the organization:

Accessibility and inclusion.

Employee well-being.

Customer and participant satisfaction.

Quality of life.

Community trust.

Autonomy and dignity.

Opportunities for participation.

Improvements in the problem the organization seeks to address.

Neither category should automatically substitute for the other.

An organization should not claim success solely because it is profitable, nor claim financial sustainability solely because its mission is beneficial.

The model encourages transparent reporting of both.

6. The Barkly Replication Framework

The Barkly Model is intended to be adopted beyond organizations owned or operated by Barkly itself.

Adoption

An organization may adopt the model by:

Reviewing the foundational principles.

Evaluating its existing organizational practices.

Identifying areas of alignment and improvement.

Developing an implementation plan.

Establishing measurable objectives.

Evaluating outcomes over time.

Adaptation

The model should not require every organization to operate identically.

A scientific laboratory, housing organization, automotive enterprise, and community food program have different requirements.

Organizations may adapt the operational components while documenting how they interpret and implement the foundational principles.

Documentation

Participating organizations are encouraged to publish:

Their implementation approach.

Organizational policies and practices.

Relevant economic outcomes.

Human and community impact.

Challenges and failures.

Lessons learned.

Proposed improvements to the framework.

Independence

Organizations adopting the model do not automatically become owned by, controlled by, or legally affiliated with Barkly Labs.

The framework should be capable of spreading independently.

7. The Barkly Evidence Standard

The Barkly Model's claims should be supported by evidence rather than assumption.

Research objectives

Investigate whether and how human-centered organizational practices influence:

Financial performance.

Employee well-being and retention.

Operational efficiency.

Customer relationships.

Innovation.

Community outcomes.

Organizational resilience.

Research methods

Depending on the context:

Longitudinal studies.

Comparative organizational analysis.

Employee and participant surveys.

Financial performance analysis.

Qualitative interviews.

Independent evaluations.

Published case studies.

Transparency

Reports should distinguish between:

Demonstrated results.

Preliminary findings.

Correlations.

Causal evidence.

Unverified hypotheses.

Limitations and uncertainty.

The objective is to build credible evidence that others can independently evaluate.

9. The Barkly Model as an Open Framework

The framework should evolve through a structured process.

Versioning

v0.1: Foundational principles and initial concept.

v0.2: Organizational design and operational guidelines.

v0.3: Measurement and evaluation standards.

v0.4: Pilot implementation and documented case studies.

v1.0: Initial mature, publicly documented framework.

v1.x: Iterative improvements and refinements.

v2.0: Major revisions supported by accumulated research and experience.

Community contributions

Future contributors may:

Propose revisions.

Submit implementation case studies.

Identify problems and contradictions.

Suggest accessibility improvements.

Develop industry-specific modules.

Contribute research and evaluation.

Document alternative approaches.

Governance

As the framework develops, Barkly should establish transparent processes for reviewing contributions, evaluating proposed changes, resolving disagreements, and maintaining the integrity of its foundational principles.

10. The Ultimate Objective

The Barkly Model seeks to demonstrate that a different approach to organizational design is possible.

It aims to establish that organizations can pursue financial sustainability and competitive performance while recognizing human dignity, accessibility, community well-being, and ethical responsibility as fundamental considerations.

Its success would extend beyond Barkly's own organizations.

If other enterprises independently adopt, adapt, and improve the model, the underlying principles could influence organizational practices across industries and countries.

The ultimate goal is not for every organization to become Barkly.

The goal is for organizations everywhere to become better at serving the people whose lives they affect.

Foundational Statement

The Barkly Model is a framework for designing organizations that pursue economic sustainability while prioritizing human dignity, accessibility, autonomy, community well-being, and responsible innovation.

We seek to demonstrate, measure, and share the possibility of a more compassionate approach to organizational design.

We believe that better systems can produce better outcomes.

We believe that innovation should serve people.

We believe that economic activity can contribute to a healthier society.

We believe that organizational practices can evolve through evidence, collaboration, and shared learning.

And we believe that the principles of a better world should be reflected in the systems we build today.

People are people, not numbers.
